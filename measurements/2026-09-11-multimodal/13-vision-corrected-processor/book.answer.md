**1. Summary**

Charles Dickens’ *A Tale of Two Cities* is set against the backdrop of the French Revolution, contrasting the social unrest in Paris with the relative stability of London. The narrative centers on Dr. Alexandre Manette, a physician who has been imprisoned in the Bastille for eighteen years without trial. He is "recalled to life" by his daughter, Lucie, whom he believed dead. Lucie, raised in England, travels to Paris with Mr. Jarvis Lorry, a banker at Tellson’s, to retrieve her father. They return to London, where Dr. Manette slowly recovers his sanity, aided by Lucie’s love, though he occasionally relapses into shoemaking, a trauma response from his imprisonment.

In London, Lucie meets Charles Darnay, a French aristocrat who has renounced his title and family name (St. Evrémonde) due to his horror at his family’s cruelty. Darnay is falsely accused of treason in England but is acquitted largely due to the intervention of Sydney Carton, a brilliant but dissolute English barrister who bears a striking physical resemblance to Darnay. Carton falls deeply in love with Lucie, who marries Darnay. Carton accepts his rejection with dignity, promising to do anything for Lucie and those she loves.

The Revolution erupts in France. The Marquis St. Evrémonde, Darnay’s uncle, is assassinated by the revolutionary Jacques, marking the beginning of the terror. Darnay receives a letter from his former servant, Gabelle, who is imprisoned for serving an emigrant. Feeling responsible, Darnay travels to Paris to help him, despite warnings. Upon arrival, he is arrested as an emigrant and aristocrat. Dr. Manette, Lucie, and Mr. Lorry follow to Paris. Dr. Manette’s status as a former Bastille prisoner grants him influence, and he initially secures Darnay’s release. However, the Defarges, wine-shop owners and revolutionary leaders, denounce Darnay. Madame Defarge, whose family was destroyed by the Evrémonde brothers, reveals a letter written by Dr. Manette during his imprisonment. In it, Manette denounces the Evrémonde family to the grave, effectively sealing Darnay’s fate.

Darnay is sentenced to death. Sydney Carton arrives in Paris, having discovered the resemblance between himself and Darnay can be exploited. He drugs Darnay, swaps clothes with him, and uses his influence with the spy John Barsad to have Darnay carried out of the prison in a swoon. Carton takes Darnay’s place in the cell. Madame Defarge goes to Lucie’s lodgings to arrest her, but is intercepted by Miss Pross, Lucie’s devoted servant. In a violent struggle, Madame Defarge’s pistol discharges, killing her instantly. Miss Pross, deafened by the shot, escapes with the others.

Carton, disguised as Darnay, rides in the tumbril to the guillotine. He comforts a fellow prisoner, a seamstress, and faces death with peace. The novel ends with Carton’s prophetic vision of a future where Lucie’s son, named after him, lives a virtuous life, and his sacrifice brings peace to the families he loved.

**2. Brief Answers**

*   **Who marries Lucie?** Charles Darnay.
*   **Who goes to the guillotine in whose place?** Sydney Carton goes in Charles Darnay’s place.
*   **What motivates that sacrifice?** Carton’s unrequited love for Lucie and his desire to redeem his wasted life through a noble act.
*   **What happens to Madame Defarge?** She is killed by her own pistol during a struggle with Miss Pross.
*   **What is Jerry Cruncher's secret occupation?** He is a "Resurrection-Man" (grave robber/body snatcher).
*   **What is Dr Manette's prison designation?** One Hundred and Five, North Tower.
*   **How is the Marquis connected to Darnay?** The Marquis is Darnay’s uncle.

**4. Quote and Explanation**

> "It is a far, far better thing that I do, than I have ever done; it is a far, far better rest that I go to than I have ever known."

This quote supports the summary by encapsulating the novel’s central theme of redemption through sacrifice. It confirms that Sydney Carton’s final act is not merely a plot device to save Darnay, but the culmination of his character arc. It transforms him from a "disappointed drudge" who cared for no one into a hero who finds purpose and peace in dying for the happiness of others, fulfilling the promise he made to Lucie earlier in the story.
