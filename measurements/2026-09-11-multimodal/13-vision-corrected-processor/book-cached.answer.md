**1. Summary**

Charles Dickens’ *A Tale of Two Cities* is set against the backdrop of the French Revolution, contrasting the social unrest in Paris with the relative stability of London. The narrative centers on Dr. Alexandre Manette, a physician who has been secretly imprisoned in the Bastille for eighteen years. His daughter, Lucie, whom he believed dead, is brought to him by Mr. Jarvis Lorry of Tellson’s Bank. Lucie’s love and devotion help restore Dr. Manette’s sanity, though he remains psychologically scarred, often reverting to the trauma of his imprisonment by making shoes.

In London, Lucie meets Charles Darnay, a French aristocrat who has renounced his title and family name (St. Evrémonde) due to his horror at his family’s cruelty. Darnay is falsely accused of treason but is acquitted largely due to the intervention of Sydney Carton, a brilliant but dissolute English barrister who bears a striking physical resemblance to Darnay. Carton falls deeply in love with Lucie, who marries Darnay. Carton accepts his rejection with grace, promising to do anything for Lucie and those she loves.

Meanwhile, in Paris, the revolutionary fervor grows. The Defarges, wine-shop owners Ernest and Thérèse, lead the mob. Thérèse Defarge, whose family was destroyed by the Evrémonde brothers, knits a secret register of those condemned to die. When Darnay returns to France to save his former servant, Gabelle, he is arrested as an emigrant aristocrat. Despite Dr. Manette’s influence as a former Bastille prisoner, Darnay is condemned to death. The turning point occurs when Dr. Manette’s hidden letter, written during his imprisonment, is read in court. It reveals that the Evrémonde brothers were responsible for the death of a peasant girl and her brother, and that Dr. Manette had denounced them. This revelation seals Darnay’s fate, as the revolutionaries view the Evrémonde line as inherently guilty.

Sydney Carton, realizing the hopelessness of the situation, devises a plan. He had previously secured access to the prison through the spy John Barsad. On the night before the execution, Carton drugs Darnay, exchanges clothes with him, and has Barsad carry the unconscious Darnay out of the prison. Carton takes Darnay’s place in the cell. As the tumbrils roll to the guillotine, Carton comforts a fellow prisoner, a seamstress, and faces death with peace. He sees a vision of a future where Lucie’s child bears his name and lives a life of virtue. Carton is executed, fulfilling his promise to Lucie. Madame Defarge, who had come to arrest Lucie, is killed in a struggle with Miss Pross, Lucie’s loyal servant. The novel ends with Carton’s prophetic vision of redemption and the hope that his sacrifice will bring peace to the living.

**2. Brief Answers**

*   **Who marries Lucie?** Charles Darnay.
*   **Who goes to the guillotine in whose place?** Sydney Carton goes in Charles Darnay’s place.
*   **What motivates that sacrifice?** Carton’s unrequited love for Lucie and his desire to redeem his wasted life through a noble act.
*   **What happens to Madame Defarge?** She is killed in a struggle with Miss Pross; her own pistol discharges, killing her.
*   **What is Jerry Cruncher's secret occupation?** He is a "Resurrection-Man" (grave robber/body snatcher).
*   **What is Dr Manette's prison designation?** One Hundred and Five, North Tower.
*   **How is the Marquis connected to Darnay?** The Marquis St. Evrémonde is Darnay’s uncle.

**4. Quote and Explanation**

**Quote:**
> "It is a far, far better thing that I do, than I have ever done; it is a far, far better rest that I go to than I have ever known."

**Explanation:**
This final line encapsulates the novel’s central theme of resurrection and redemption. Throughout the book, Carton is described as a man who has "wasted" his life and talents. By sacrificing himself for Darnay, Carton achieves the moral purpose he lacked in life. The quote confirms the summary’s assertion that Carton’s death is not a defeat, but a triumphant act of love that secures the future happiness of Lucie’s family and provides Carton with spiritual peace, fulfilling the novel’s title motif of being "recalled to life."
