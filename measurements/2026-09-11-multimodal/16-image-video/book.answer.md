**1. Summary**

Charles Dickens’ *A Tale of Two Cities* is set against the backdrop of the French Revolution, contrasting the social unrest in Paris with the relative stability of London. The narrative begins in 1775 with Mr. Jarvis Lorry, a banker at Tellson’s, traveling to Paris to retrieve Dr. Alexandre Manette, who has been imprisoned in the Bastille for eighteen years. Dr. Manette is mentally shattered, reduced to a shoemaker known only as "One Hundred and Five, North Tower." His daughter, Lucie, whom he believed dead, nurses him back to health, and they relocate to London.

Five years later, Charles Darnay, a French aristocrat who has renounced his title and family name (St. Evrémonde) due to his family’s cruelty, is tried for treason in London. He is acquitted largely due to the intervention of Sydney Carton, a brilliant but dissolute English barrister who resembles Darnay. Carton falls deeply in love with Lucie, who marries Darnay. Carton accepts his rejection with dignity, promising to do anything for Lucie and those she loves.

Meanwhile, in Paris, the Defarges, wine-shop owners and revolutionaries, plot against the aristocracy. Madame Defarge knits a secret register of those condemned to die. The Marquis St. Evrémonde, Darnay’s uncle, is assassinated by Gaspard, a peasant whose child was killed by the Marquis’s carriage. This act ignites further tension. When the Revolution erupts in 1789, the Bastille falls, and the Defarges discover Dr. Manette’s hidden letter. The letter reveals that the Evrémonde brothers had imprisoned Manette to silence him after he witnessed their crimes against a peasant family, which included the rape and death of a young woman and the murder of her brother.

Years later, Darnay returns to Paris to save his former servant, Gabelle, who is imprisoned. Despite Dr. Manette’s influence, Darnay is arrested as an emigrant aristocrat. He is initially acquitted due to Manette’s testimony, but Madame Defarge denounces him, citing the Evrémonde family’s crimes. Dr. Manette, unaware that his own letter condemns Darnay, inadvertently seals his son-in-law’s fate. Darnay is sentenced to death.

Sydney Carton, realizing the hopelessness of the situation, devises a plan. He bribes John Barsad, a spy and turnkey, to gain access to Darnay’s cell. Using his physical resemblance to Darnay, Carton drugs the prisoner, swaps clothes with him, and has Barsad carry the unconscious Darnay out of the prison. Carton takes Darnay’s place on the tumbril to the guillotine. He dies alongside a young seamstress who comforts him in his final moments.

Madame Defarge, seeking to kill Lucie and her child, confronts Miss Pross, Lucie’s devoted servant. In a violent struggle, a pistol discharges, killing Madame Defarge. Miss Pross, deafened by the shot, escapes with the family. The novel ends with Carton’s prophetic vision of a future where Lucie’s son carries his name, and his sacrifice brings peace and redemption, declaring it "a far, far better thing that I do, than I have ever done."

**2. Brief Answers**

*   **Who marries Lucie?** Charles Darnay.
*   **Who goes to the guillotine in whose place?** Sydney Carton goes in Charles Darnay’s place.
*   **What motivates that sacrifice?** Carton’s unrequited love for Lucie and his desire to redeem his wasted life through a noble act.
*   **What happens to Madame Defarge?** She is killed during a struggle with Miss Pross when her own pistol discharges.
*   **What is Jerry Cruncher's secret occupation?** He is a "Resurrection-Man" (grave robber/body snatcher).
*   **What is Dr Manette's prison designation?** One Hundred and Five, North Tower.
*   **How is the Marquis connected to Darnay?** The Marquis is Darnay’s uncle.

**4. Quote and Explanation**

**Quote:** "It is a far, far better thing that I do, than I have ever done; it is a far, far better rest that I go to than I have ever known."

**Explanation:** This final line encapsulates the novel’s central theme of resurrection and redemption. Throughout the story, Carton is described as a man who has wasted his potential, living a life of "sloth and sensuality." By sacrificing himself for Darnay, Carton transforms his meaningless existence into a purposeful act of love. The quote confirms the summary’s assertion that his death is not a defeat, but a spiritual victory that secures the happiness of Lucie’s family and grants Carton the peace he never found in life.
