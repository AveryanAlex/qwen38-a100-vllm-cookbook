**1. Summary**

Charles Dickens’ *A Tale of Two Cities* is set against the backdrop of the French Revolution, contrasting the social unrest in Paris with the relative stability of London. The narrative begins in 1775, when Jarvis Lorry, a banker at Tellson’s, travels to Paris to inform Lucie Manette that her father, Dr. Alexandre Manette, is alive after being imprisoned in the Bastille for eighteen years. Dr. Manette, traumatized by his captivity, has been reduced to a shoemaker known only as "One Hundred and Five, North Tower." Lucie, raised in England believing her father dead, travels to Paris to reclaim him. Her love and devotion help restore his sanity, and they return to London.

Five years later, Charles Darnay, a French aristocrat who has renounced his title and family name (St. Evrémonde) due to his family’s cruelty, is tried for treason in London. He is acquitted largely due to the intervention of Sydney Carton, a brilliant but dissolute English barrister who resembles Darnay. Carton falls deeply in love with Lucie, who marries Darnay. Carton accepts his role as a peripheral friend, promising to sacrifice himself for Lucie and those she loves.

Meanwhile, in France, the oppression of the peasantry by the aristocracy culminates in the storming of the Bastille. Ernest Defarge, a wine-shop keeper, and his wife, Madame Defarge, lead the revolutionary movement. Madame Defarge knits a secret register of those condemned to die, driven by a personal vendetta against the Evrémonde family, who destroyed her own. Dr. Manette’s past imprisonment is revealed to be the result of a letter he wrote denouncing the Evrémonde brothers for their crimes against the Defarge family.

Years later, Darnay returns to France to save his former servant, Gabelle, despite warnings. He is arrested as an emigrant aristocrat. Although Dr. Manette’s status as a former Bastille prisoner initially secures Darnay’s release, Madame Defarge denounces him, citing the Evrémonde lineage and Dr. Manette’s own damning letter. Darnay is sentenced to death.

Sydney Carton arrives in Paris and discovers that his resemblance to Darnay can be exploited. He drugs Darnay, exchanges clothes with him, and has Darnay carried out of the prison in a coffin-like litter. Carton takes Darnay’s place in the cell. As the tumbrils roll toward the guillotine, Carton comforts a young seamstress, sharing her fate. He is executed, fulfilling his promise to Lucie. The novel ends with Carton’s prophetic vision of a future where Lucie’s son carries his name, and his sacrifice brings peace and redemption. The story illustrates the destructive power of vengeance and the redemptive power of selfless love, suggesting that while the Revolution was born of justified anger, its excesses required a moral counterbalance.

**2. Brief Answers**

*   **Who marries Lucie?** Charles Darnay.
*   **Who goes to the guillotine in whose place?** Sydney Carton goes in Charles Darnay’s place.
*   **What motivates that sacrifice?** Carton’s unrequited love for Lucie and his desire to redeem his wasted life through a noble act.
*   **What happens to Madame Defarge?** She is killed in a struggle with Miss Pross; her own pistol discharges, killing her.
*   **What is Jerry Cruncher's secret occupation?** He is a "Resurrection-Man" (grave robber/body snatcher).
*   **What is Dr Manette's prison designation?** One Hundred and Five, North Tower.
*   **How is the Marquis connected to Darnay?** The Marquis St. Evrémonde is Darnay’s uncle.

**4. Quote and Explanation**

**Quote:**
> "It is a far, far better thing that I do, than I have ever done; it is a far, far better rest that I go to than I have ever known."

**Explanation:**
This final line encapsulates the novel’s central theme of redemption through sacrifice. Throughout the story, Sydney Carton is portrayed as a man who has wasted his potential, describing himself as a "disappointed drudge." By taking Darnay’s place, Carton transforms his life’s meaning. The quote confirms that his death is not a defeat, but a triumphant act of love that secures the happiness of Lucie, Darnay, and their child. It supports the summary’s assertion that the novel contrasts the destructive cycle of revolutionary vengeance with the constructive, redemptive power of individual selflessness.
