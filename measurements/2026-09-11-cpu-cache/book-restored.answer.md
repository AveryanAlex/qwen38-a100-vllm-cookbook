Charles Dickens’ *A Tale of Two Cities* is set against the backdrop of the French Revolution, contrasting the social unrest in Paris with the relative stability of London. The story begins in 1775 when Mr. Jarvis Lorry, a banker at Tellson’s Bank, travels to Paris to retrieve Dr. Alexandre Manette, who has been imprisoned in the Bastille for eighteen years. Dr. Manette is mentally shattered, reduced to a shoemaker known only as "One Hundred and Five, North Tower." He is rescued by his daughter, Lucie, and brought to England, where Lucie’s love helps restore his sanity.

Five years later, Charles Darnay, a French aristocrat who has renounced his title and family name (St. Evrémonde) due to his horror at his family’s oppression of the peasantry, is tried for treason in London. He is acquitted largely due to the intervention of Sydney Carton, a brilliant but alcoholic and cynical English barrister who resembles Darnay. Carton falls deeply in love with Lucie, who marries Darnay. Carton promises to sacrifice his life for Lucie and those she loves.

Meanwhile, in Paris, the Defarges, wine-shop owners and leaders of the revolutionary movement, plot against the aristocracy. Madame Defarge knits a secret register of those condemned to die. The Marquis St. Evrémonde, Darnay’s uncle, is assassinated by Gaspard, a peasant whose child was killed by the Marquis’s carriage. The Revolution erupts in 1789 with the storming of the Bastille. Darnay returns to France to save his former servant, Gabelle, but is arrested as an emigrant aristocrat. Dr. Manette uses his influence as a former Bastille prisoner to secure Darnay’s release, but Darnay is rearrested.

At Darnay’s second trial, Madame Defarge reveals a letter written by Dr. Manette during his imprisonment. The letter denounces the Evrémonde family for their crimes against the Defarge family, specifically the rape and murder of Madame Defarge’s sister and the death of her brother. This testimony seals Darnay’s fate. Dr. Manette, overwhelmed by the revelation that his own suffering was caused by the Evrémondes, suffers a relapse into his shoemaking trance.

Sydney Carton, having secured access to the prison through a spy, visits Darnay. He drugs Darnay, exchanges clothes with him, and has Darnay carried out of the prison in a carriage with Lucie, Dr. Manette, and Mr. Lorry. Carton takes Darnay’s place on the tumbril to the guillotine. He dies alongside a young seamstress, finding peace and purpose in his sacrifice. Madame Defarge, seeking to arrest Lucie, confronts Miss Pross, Lucie’s devoted servant. In the struggle, Madame Defarge’s pistol discharges, killing her instantly. Miss Pross is left deaf from the gunshot. The novel ends with Carton’s prophetic vision of a better future and his famous declaration that his sacrifice is "a far, far better thing" than he has ever done.

**Answers to Specific Questions:**

*   **Who marries Lucie?** Charles Darnay.
*   **Who goes to the guillotine in whose place?** Sydney Carton goes to the guillotine in the place of Charles Darnay.
*   **What motivates that sacrifice?** Carton’s unrequited love for Lucie and his desire to redeem his wasted life through a noble act.
*   **What happens to Madame Defarge?** She is killed by her own pistol during a struggle with Miss Pross.
*   **What is Jerry Cruncher's secret occupation?** He is a "Resurrection-Man" (grave robber/body snatcher).
*   **What is Dr Manette's prison designation?** One Hundred and Five, North Tower.
*   **How is the Marquis connected to Darnay?** The Marquis St. Evrémonde is Darnay’s uncle.
