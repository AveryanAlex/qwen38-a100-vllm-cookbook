**1. Summary**

Charles Dickens’ *A Tale of Two Cities* is set against the backdrop of the French Revolution, contrasting the social unrest in Paris with the relative stability of London. The narrative centers on Dr. Alexandre Manette, a physician who has been secretly imprisoned in the Bastille for eighteen years. His release is orchestrated by Mr. Jarvis Lorry, a banker at Tellson’s, who reunites Manette with his daughter, Lucie, whom he had believed dead. Lucie’s love and devotion help restore Manette’s sanity, though he remains psychologically scarred, occasionally reverting to his prison habit of shoemaking.

In London, Charles Darnay, a French aristocrat who has renounced his title and family name (St. Evrémonde) due to his family’s cruelty, is tried for treason. He is acquitted largely due to the intervention of Sydney Carton, a brilliant but dissolute English barrister who bears a striking physical resemblance to Darnay. Carton falls deeply in love with Lucie, who marries Darnay. Carton accepts his rejection with grace, promising to do anything for Lucie and those she loves.

Meanwhile, in Paris, the revolutionary fervor intensifies. Ernest Defarge, a wine-shop keeper, and his wife, Madame Defarge, lead the Jacquerie. Madame Defarge knits a secret register of those condemned to die, driven by a personal vendetta against the Evrémonde family, who destroyed her own. When Darnay returns to France to save his former servant, Gabelle, he is arrested as an emigrant. Despite Dr. Manette’s influence as a former Bastille prisoner, Darnay is condemned to death after Madame Defarge produces a letter Manette wrote in prison, denouncing the Evrémondes for their crimes against her family.

Sydney Carton arrives in Paris and discovers that Madame Defarge intends to denounce Lucie and her child as well. To save the family, Carton devises a plan. He drugs Darnay, exchanges clothes with him, and has Darnay carried out of the prison in a swoon, while Carton takes his place in the cell. Carton, knowing his own life is forfeit, chooses to die so that Lucie’s happiness may survive. He is executed alongside a young seamstress who finds comfort in his presence. Madame Defarge, attempting to arrest Lucie, is killed in a struggle with Miss Pross, Lucie’s devoted servant. The novel concludes with Carton’s prophetic vision of a future where his sacrifice brings peace and prosperity to Lucie’s descendants, declaring his act "a far, far better thing" than he has ever done.

**2. Brief Answers**

*   **Who marries Lucie?** Charles Darnay.
*   **Who goes to the guillotine in whose place?** Sydney Carton goes in Charles Darnay’s place.
*   **What motivates that sacrifice?** Carton’s unrequited love for Lucie and his desire to redeem his wasted life by saving those she loves.
*   **What happens to Madame Defarge?** She is killed in a struggle with Miss Pross; her own pistol discharges, killing her.
*   **What is Jerry Cruncher's secret occupation?** He is a "Resurrection-Man" (grave robber/body snatcher).
*   **What is Dr Manette's prison designation?** One Hundred and Five, North Tower.
*   **How is the Marquis connected to Darnay?** The Marquis St. Evrémonde is Darnay’s uncle.

**4. Quote and Explanation**

*Quote:* "It is a far, far better thing that I do, than I have ever done; it is a far, far better rest that I go to than I have ever known."

*Explanation:* This final line encapsulates the novel’s central theme of redemption through sacrifice. It supports the summary by confirming that Carton’s death is not a defeat, but a triumphant act of love that gives meaning to his previously "wasted" life. It validates the causal chain of the plot: his earlier promise to Lucie ("I would give my life, to keep a life you love beside you") is fulfilled, securing the future of the Manette-Darnay family and providing the "peaceful, useful, prosperous and happy" life he envisions for them.
