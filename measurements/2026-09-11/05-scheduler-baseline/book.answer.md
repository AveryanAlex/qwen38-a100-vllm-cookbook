**1. Summary**

Charles Dickens’ *A Tale of Two Cities* is set against the backdrop of the French Revolution, contrasting the social unrest in Paris with the relative stability of London. The narrative begins in 1775 with Mr. Jarvis Lorry, a banker at Tellson’s, traveling to Paris to retrieve Dr. Alexandre Manette, who has been secretly imprisoned in the Bastille for eighteen years. Dr. Manette, traumatized by his captivity, is now a shoemaker. He is rescued by his daughter, Lucie, whom he had believed dead. They return to London, where Lucie’s love helps restore her father’s sanity.

Five years later, Charles Darnay, a French aristocrat who has renounced his title and family name (St. Evrémonde) due to his family’s cruelty, is tried for treason in London. He is acquitted largely due to the intervention of Sydney Carton, a brilliant but dissolute English lawyer who bears a striking physical resemblance to Darnay. Carton falls deeply in love with Lucie, who marries Darnay. Carton accepts his rejection with dignity, promising to do anything for Lucie and those she loves.

As the French Revolution erupts, the Defarges, wine-shop owners in Paris, lead the uprising. Madame Defarge knits a register of those condemned to die, driven by a personal vendetta against the Evrémonde family, who destroyed her own. Darnay returns to France to save his former servant, Gabelle, but is arrested as an emigrant aristocrat. Dr. Manette, Lucie, and Mr. Lorry follow him to Paris. Initially, Manette’s status as a former Bastille prisoner grants him influence, and Darnay is briefly released. However, Madame Defarge denounces Darnay, revealing that Manette’s own written testimony, found in the Bastille, details the Evrémonde brothers’ crimes against her family. Darnay is sentenced to death.

Sydney Carton arrives in Paris and discovers that the Defarges also plan to execute Lucie and her child. He realizes that his resemblance to Darnay is the key to saving the family. He drugs Darnay, exchanges clothes with him, and has the spy Barsad carry the unconscious Darnay out of the prison. Carton takes Darnay’s place in the cell. Meanwhile, Miss Pross, Lucie’s devoted servant, confronts Madame Defarge, who has come to arrest Lucie. In a struggle, Madame Defarge’s pistol discharges, killing her instantly. Miss Pross, deafened by the shot, escapes with the family.

Carton, disguised as Darnay, rides in the tumbril to the guillotine alongside a young seamstress who comforts him. He dies with a sense of peace and prophetic vision, believing his sacrifice will bring happiness to Lucie’s family and future generations. The novel ends with Carton’s famous reflection that his act is "a far, far better thing" than he has ever done, securing the safety of those he loves and offering a vision of redemption amidst the terror of the Revolution.

**2. Brief Answers**

*   **Who marries Lucie?** Charles Darnay.
*   **Who goes to the guillotine in whose place?** Sydney Carton goes in Charles Darnay’s place.
*   **What motivates that sacrifice?** Carton’s unrequited love for Lucie and his desire to redeem his wasted life by saving those she loves.
*   **What happens to Madame Defarge?** She is killed by her own pistol during a struggle with Miss Pross.
*   **What is Jerry Cruncher's secret occupation?** He is a "Resurrection-Man" (grave robber/body snatcher).
*   **What is Dr Manette's prison designation?** One Hundred and Five, North Tower.
*   **How is the Marquis connected to Darnay?** The Marquis St. Evrémonde is Darnay’s uncle.

**4. Quote and Explanation**

> "It is a far, far better thing that I do, than I have ever done; it is a far, far better rest that I go to than I have ever known."

This quote supports the summary by encapsulating the novel’s central theme of redemption through sacrifice. It confirms that Carton’s death is not a defeat, but a triumphant act of love that gives meaning to his previously "wasted" life. It also validates the summary’s assertion that his sacrifice secures the future happiness of Lucie’s family, providing the "peaceful, useful, prosperous and happy" life he envisions in his final thoughts.
