**1. Summary**

Charles Dickens’ *A Tale of Two Cities* is set against the backdrop of the French Revolution, contrasting the social unrest in Paris with the relative stability of London. The narrative begins in 1775 with Mr. Jarvis Lorry, a banker at Tellson’s, traveling to Paris to retrieve Dr. Alexandre Manette, who has been secretly imprisoned in the Bastille for eighteen years. Dr. Manette, traumatized by his captivity, is reduced to a shoemaker. He is rescued by his daughter, Lucie, whom he had believed dead, and they return to England.

Five years later, Lucie and her father attend the trial of Charles Darnay, a French aristocrat accused of treason in England. Darnay is acquitted largely due to the intervention of Sydney Carton, a brilliant but dissolute English barrister who resembles Darnay. Carton falls deeply in love with Lucie, though she marries Darnay. Darnay, having renounced his cruel aristocratic heritage, lives modestly in London as a French tutor.

The Revolution erupts in France. The Marquis St. Evrémonde, Darnay’s uncle, is assassinated by Jacques, a revolutionary leader. Years later, Darnay receives a desperate letter from his former servant, Gabelle, who is imprisoned in Paris. Despite warnings, Darnay travels to France to help him. Upon arrival, he is arrested as an emigrant aristocrat. Lucie, Dr. Manette, and Mr. Lorry follow him to Paris.

Dr. Manette, revered as a Bastille survivor, uses his influence to secure Darnay’s release. However, the revolutionaries, led by the vengeful wine-shop keeper Ernest Defarge and his wife, Madame Defarge, denounce Darnay again. Madame Defarge reveals that her family was destroyed by the Evrémonde brothers (Darnay’s father and uncle). She presents a letter written by Dr. Manette during his imprisonment, which denounces the Evrémonde family to the grave. This document, which Manette had forgotten, seals Darnay’s fate. He is sentenced to death by the guillotine.

Sydney Carton, who has followed the family to Paris, devises a plan to save Darnay. He bribes John Barsad, a spy and turnkey, to allow him access to Darnay’s cell. Carton drugs Darnay, exchanges clothes with him, and has Barsad carry the unconscious Darnay out of the prison. Carton, disguised as Darnay, takes his place in the tumbril.

Madame Defarge goes to the Manette residence to arrest Lucie, but is intercepted by Miss Pross, Lucie’s devoted English servant. In a violent struggle, Madame Defarge’s pistol discharges, killing her instantly. Miss Pross, deafened by the gunshot, escapes with Jerry Cruncher.

Carton and a fellow victim, a seamstress, ride to the guillotine. Carton comforts her, and they are executed together. The novel ends with Carton’s prophetic vision of a future where Lucie and Darnay’s descendants live in peace, and his name is honored. His sacrifice redeems his wasted life, fulfilling his promise to Lucie that he would give his life to keep a life she loves beside her.

**2. Brief Answers**

*   **Who marries Lucie?** Charles Darnay.
*   **Who goes to the guillotine in whose place?** Sydney Carton goes in Charles Darnay’s place.
*   **What motivates that sacrifice?** Carton’s unrequited love for Lucie and his desire to redeem his wasted life through a noble act.
*   **What happens to Madame Defarge?** She is killed by her own pistol during a struggle with Miss Pross.
*   **What is Jerry Cruncher's secret occupation?** He is a "Resurrection-Man" (grave robber/body snatcher).
*   **What is Dr Manette's prison designation?** One Hundred and Five, North Tower.
*   **How is the Marquis connected to Darnay?** The Marquis is Darnay’s uncle.

**4. Quote and Explanation**

*Quote:* "It is a far, far better thing that I do, than I have ever done; it is a far, far better rest that I go to than I have ever known."

*Explanation:* This final line encapsulates the novel’s central theme of redemption through sacrifice. It supports the summary by confirming that Carton’s death is not a defeat, but a triumphant moral victory. It validates the summary’s assertion that Carton’s act redeems his previously "dissolute" and "wasted" life, providing the "peacefullest" and most meaningful conclusion to his character arc.
